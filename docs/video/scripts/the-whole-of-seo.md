#### What is a search engine?

*\[Face, medium framing.\]*

When most people hear the phrase "search engine", they immediately think of Google.

And Google is obviously one of the biggest examples.

But the underlying behaviour is much broader than Google.

*\[Screen: quick cuts of search bars. YouTube, Amazon, Instagram, Facebook, Google Maps, a marketplace, a website's own search box.\]*

*\[Back to face.\]*

So I want you to separate two ideas.

The first is search as a behaviour.

The second is a particular search engine or search platform.

Search is simply the act of trying to find something.

And the platform is the system helping you find it.

That distinction matters because SEO has become much broader than the old idea of:

"Put some keywords on your website and get to number one on Google."

That's not the whole thing.

SEO is about discoverability.

It's about helping the systems people use to find information understand what you have, who it's for, where it applies, and why it might be useful.

A website page.

A product page.

A business profile.

A video.

a person who wants something

information that might satisfy that need.

SEO sits in the middle.

You're not.

You're building something for a person.

Then you're making it easier for search systems to understand and surface it.

So here's the definition I want you to remember:

SEO is the work of making the things you want people to find discoverable in the places where they search.

### Part Three: The searcher

*\[Chapter card: PART THREE / THE SEARCHER\]*

#### Before SEO: what does the person actually want?

Now let's talk about one of the most important concepts in all of SEO.

Search intent.

Because a keyword is not a customer.

A search query is a clue about what the person wants.

⁺Through a lot of this video, I'm going to use one real business as the example.

⁺My own.

⁺Jortech. We repair phones, tablets, laptops and consoles, and we recover data.

*\[Lower-third: JORTECH, TECH REPAIR, JORTECH.CO.UK\]*

*\[Screen: a search box. Type each query as it is spoken.\]*

Imagine someone searches:

"phone screen"

What do they want?

We don't know.

They might want to buy a replacement screen.

They might want to fix it themselves.

They might want to know what a repair costs.

They might want a screen protector.

They might be looking for a repair shop.

They might be looking for one near them.

Now compare that with:

"cracked iPhone screen repair near me"

That's much more specific.

Or:

"how to fix a phone that won't charge"

Different intent.

Or:

"iPhone 15 screen replacement cost"

Different again.

Or:

"Jortech phone number"

That's navigational.

The person already knows what they're looking for.

⁺They already know the business by name. They just need the way in.

So you can have the same broad subject and completely different intent.

And if you misunderstand the intent, you can create a technically excellent page that is still the wrong answer.

That's one of the biggest mistakes in SEO.

People ask:

"What keyword should I rank for?"

A better question is:

*\[Text on screen.\]*

"What is the person actually trying to accomplish?"

#### Short-tail and long-tail search terms

You'll often hear SEO people talk about short-tail and long-tail keywords.

A short-tail term might be:

"phone repair"

"dentist"

"running shoes"

"SEO"

A long-tail search might be:

"iPhone screen repair open now in Leeds"

"best running shoes for flat feet under £100"

"how to fix a WordPress canonical error"

Long-tail doesn't necessarily mean "better."

And short-tail doesn't necessarily mean "worse."

They represent different levels of specificity.

The more specific the search, often the clearer the person's intent becomes.

But there is another important lesson here.

Search volume is not the same thing as business value.

A keyword with 100,000 searches isn't automatically more valuable to your business than a keyword with 500.

If those 500 people are looking for exactly what you sell, the smaller audience may matter enormously.

SEO isn't a popularity contest.

It's about matching demand with relevance.

#### Keyword research

Keywords still matter.

Anyone telling you that words people type into search don't matter is making this more complicated than it needs to be.

Search engines need to understand what a page is about.

And users need to recognise that your result is relevant to what they're looking for.

But modern SEO shouldn't mean repeating the same phrase 47 times.

You start with the subject.

Then you understand the different ways people describe it.

Then you understand the questions around it.

Then you understand the different intents.

Then you build useful resources around those needs.

For example, take a tech repair business like Jortech.

*\[Graphic: a demand map builds. "Tech repair" in the centre, services branching off, questions hanging off each service.\]*

You might have:

Phone screen repair.

Battery replacement.

Laptop repair.

Console repair.

Water damage repair.

Data recovery.

And questions:

How much does a phone screen repair cost?

How long does a laptop repair take?

How do I know whether my laptop is worth repairing?

What should I do if my phone gets wet?

Those aren't simply a list of keywords.

They're a map of customer problems.

That's how I want you thinking about keyword research.

*\[Text on screen.\]*

Don't build a keyword list. Build a map of demand.

This is where you provide machine-readable information about what a page represents.

For example:

An article.

### Part Five: Place two, the map

*\[Chapter card: PLACE TWO / THE MAP. Corner badge: MAP lit.\]*

The second place is:

The map.

This is particularly important for local businesses.

You want the business to be understood as:

the right business,

in the right place,

offering the right thing.

#### Local SEO

⁺This is local SEO, one of the biggest areas for local businesses.

And local search is different because location matters.

*\[Screen: the same search, "coffee shop", shown with an Amsterdam map pack, then a Manchester one.\]*

If I search:

"coffee shop"

the results I want in Amsterdam aren't the same results I want in Manchester.

If I search:

"phone screen repair"

the physical location of the business can be extremely important.

*\[On screen: Source: Google Business Profile Help, "Tips to improve your local ranking on Google"\]*

Google currently says local results are mainly based on:

relevance, distance and prominence.

Let's unpack those.

**Relevance**

*\[Graphic: RELEVANCE / DISTANCE / PROMINENCE, lighting one at a time.\]*

Does the business match what the person is looking for?

If someone searches for a dentist, a dentist is relevant.

A bicycle shop isn't.

If someone searches for console repair, a shop that actually repairs consoles is more relevant than one that only sells phone cases.

So your business information needs to make the business understandable.

**Distance**

How far is the business from the relevant location?

This can be the searcher's location or a location included in the query.

And this is important because local SEO isn't simply:

"Put the town name on your website."

Physical geography matters.

**Prominence**

Then there's prominence.

How well known is the business?

Google says prominence can be informed by things such as links from other websites and reviews, among other information.

This is where local SEO starts overlapping with wider SEO.

Your website matters.

Your reputation matters.

Other websites matter.

Your business profile matters.

Your reviews matter.

And the information Google receives from across the web can matter.

#### Google Business Profile

Your Google Business Profile is one of the obvious foundations for a local business.

*\[Screen: Jortech's Google Business Profile. Make sure every field is complete and accurate before filming.\]*

Make sure the business is represented accurately.

Business name.

Category.

Address where applicable.

Opening hours.

Phone number.

Website.

Services.

Photos.

Other relevant business information.

Google itself recommends keeping business information complete and accurate, responding to reviews, and adding photos and videos.

But don't confuse "complete profile" with:

"Google now owes me position one."

It doesn't.

A complete profile makes your business easier to understand.

It doesn't give you a guaranteed ranking.

And Google explicitly says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

*\[Hold that line on screen as a quote card.\]*

#### NAP

You'll also hear the term NAP.

Name.

Address.

Phone number.

The principle is simple:

Your business information should be accurate and coherent.

*\[Graphic: the same business on a website, a directory, a Business Profile, an old page and a social profile, each with a slightly different phone number or address. Mismatches flash.\]*

If your website says one phone number.

A directory says another.

Your business profile says something else.

An old page has an old address.

A social profile has another version.

You've created unnecessary confusion.

But don't fall into the trap of thinking there is some magical SEO superstition around whether every comma, abbreviation and punctuation mark is identical everywhere.

The important thing is accuracy and consistency of the underlying business information.

#### Local SEO matrix pages

Now let's talk about something that can be extremely powerful and extremely badly abused.

Local SEO matrix pages.

⁺These pages live on your website, but they exist to serve the map, so they belong here.

Imagine you provide:

Phone repair.

And you serve:

Leeds.

Bradford.

Wakefield.

Huddersfield.

You might naturally have pages around real service areas.

But here's the trap.

*\[Screen: four browser tabs side by side, identical pages with only the town name changed.\]*

You create:

/phone-repair-leeds

/phone-repair-bradford

/phone-repair-wakefield

/phone-repair-huddersfield

and then copy the same page four times.

You change the town name.

Everything else stays the same.

That's not a strategy.

That's a page factory.

And if the pages exist purely to capture searches while offering little unique value, you've created exactly the kind of doorway/thin-content problem you should be avoiding.

The question isn't:

"Can I make a page for this town?"

The question is:

"Does this town genuinely deserve a useful page?"

Maybe it does.

If you genuinely operate there.

If customers actually use the service there.

If you can provide meaningful local information.

If you have real examples.

If you can explain availability.

If you can show relevant projects.

If you can answer local questions.

If the page genuinely helps somebody.

Then the page can have a reason to exist.

A legitimate local content architecture might look like:

Service × Location

*\[Graphic: a grid of services by towns. Most cells stay grey; only the cells with real work, real examples and real demand light up.\]*

But every intersection has to earn its existence.

#### The map, summed up

⁺So, the map.

Google currently describes local ranking primarily through relevance, distance and prominence.

That means you should think about:

Your Business Profile.

Your categories.

Your services.

Your opening hours.

Your address where appropriate.

Your website.

Your photos.

Your reviews.

Your reputation.

Your local relevance.

And your wider presence across the web.

But remember:

Nobody can legitimately sell you a guaranteed Google Maps position.

Google itself says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

Third:

The links.

The traditional organic search results.

This is where your website pages can appear.

⁺Let's take visibility first.

#### Published is not ranked: the six stages

Now we're going to get technical.

Because before Google can rank your page, it has to be able to discover and process it.

And this gives us one of the most important distinctions in SEO:

Crawling is not indexing.

And indexing is not ranking.

And ranking is not clicking.

And clicking is not becoming a customer.

Let's slow that down.

*\[Graphic: a six-stage pipeline builds as each stage is spoken. A page icon travels along it.\]*

You publish a page.

That doesn't necessarily mean Google has crawled it.

Google discovers URLs through links, sitemaps and other mechanisms.

It then crawls the page.

It processes what it finds.

If the page is eligible and useful enough to be indexed, it may enter Google's index.

Then, when somebody searches, Google's systems decide whether that page is relevant enough to appear.

Then where it appears.

Then whether the searcher actually clicks it.

Then whether the visitor actually becomes a customer.

So:

Published.

Crawled.

Indexed.

Ranked.

Clicked.

Converted.

Those are six different things.

And confusing them causes an enormous amount of bad SEO advice.

*\[On screen: Source: Google Search Central, "In-depth guide to how Google Search works"\]*

Google itself recommends understanding crawling, indexing and serving when diagnosing Search-related issues.

⁺The machinery that gets a page crawled and indexed lives inside your website, so it belongs to the door. We'll open it up properly in Part Eight.

⁺For now, the reason to click. And that starts with two things you write yourself.

#### Page titles

Let's start with the title.

*\[Screen: a single search result, title link highlighted.\]*

The title tells users and search systems what the page is about.

A good title is descriptive.

It should represent the actual page.

It shouldn't be a collection of keywords.

If the page is:

"iPhone Screen Repair in Leeds"

then say what the page is.

Don't turn it into:

*\[Screen: the stuffed title typed out in full, visibly absurd.\]*

"Phone Phone Repair Leeds iPhone Repair Screen Repair Leeds Cheap Phone Repair Best Phone Repair Shop Leeds."

That isn't optimisation.

That's noise.

#### Meta descriptions

Then we have meta descriptions.

*\[Screen: the same result, snippet highlighted.\]*

This is another area where SEO mythology gets ahead of reality.

A meta description is not simply:

"Write this perfectly and Google will rank you higher."

Google may use the meta description as part of the search result snippet, but the displayed snippet can vary depending on the query and page content.

So think about it as search-result presentation.

Its job is to help communicate what the page offers.

It's not a secret ranking lever.

*\[Refrain tag: SIGNAL, NOT A SWITCH\]*

That's an important distinction.

### Part Seven: Place four, the reviews

Now reviews.

Reviews are one part of the picture.

And for the customer, quality and relevance matter too.

"Have they done this before?"

"What do customers say?"

#### The reviews, summed up

⁺So, the reviews.

Respond professionally.

*\[Graphic: a door with six stacked layers labelled in order. Each lights as its layer begins.\]*

Technical.

Accessibility.

the website.

⁺Let's open it, one layer at a time.

#### Layer one: technical

*\[Layer card: 1 / TECHNICAL\]*

This is technical SEO.

And I want to make something very clear.

Technical SEO is not just:

"Make your website fast."

That's one tiny part of it.

Technical SEO is about making the website accessible to search systems and users, and making the site's structure understandable and functional.

Let's go through the major pieces.

**Crawling**

A search engine needs to be able to access your content.

If a page cannot be reached, that's a problem.

If important content is hidden behind systems a crawler can't use properly, that's a problem.

If internal links don't lead to important pages, that's a problem.

If your website architecture is chaotic, that's a problem.

**Rendering**

⁺Then there's rendering.

⁺Many modern websites build parts of the page with JavaScript, in the browser.

⁺To see that content, Google has to render the page, much like a browser does.

⁺If your important content, or your links, only appear after scripts run, and something in that process fails, Google may not see what your customers see.

⁺So one simple question matters: can the important content and links be seen in the page Google actually renders? The URL Inspection tool in Search Console will show you, and we'll get to it in Part Nine.

**Robots.txt**

Then there's robots.txt.

*\[Screen: a short robots.txt file.\]*

This file can provide instructions about which areas crawlers should or shouldn't request.

But here's an important distinction:

robots.txt is not the same thing as noindex.

And blocking crawling isn't the same thing as removing a URL from search.

You have to understand what each control actually does before using it.

**Noindex**

Then there's the noindex directive.

*\[Screen: a single meta robots noindex tag in page source, highlighted.\]*

This tells compliant search engines that the page should not be included in their index.

And this is one of those tiny technical details that can have enormous consequences.

Because if you accidentally put noindex on the page you desperately want people to find…

your beautifully written page isn't going to solve that problem.

*\[Face. Slow down. This is a confession, not a lesson.\]*

I know that because I've done it.

I once had a booking page on Jortech, my own repair business, carrying a noindex directive for far too long.

*\[Screen: a recreation of the Jortech booking page, with the noindex tag highlighted in the page source. Never show real customer bookings.\]*

The page existed.

The booking system worked.

Customers could use it if they reached it.

The page wasn't the problem.

The booking system wasn't the problem.

Being found was.

Nobody complained.

Nobody emailed me saying:

"Jordan, your SEO is broken."

They simply didn't find the page.

That's an important lesson.

SEO failures aren't always dramatic.

Sometimes they're invisible.

*\[Hold one beat.\]*

**XML sitemaps**

Then we have XML sitemaps.

A sitemap can help search engines discover URLs that you want crawled.

But submitting a sitemap does not mean:

"Google, please rank all of these."

It doesn't guarantee indexing.

It doesn't guarantee ranking.

It is a discovery mechanism and a useful diagnostic resource.

Think of it as handing the search engine a map of URLs that matter.

Not a command to rank them.

**Canonicals**

Then we get to canonical URLs.

Suppose the same or substantially similar content is accessible through multiple URLs.

*\[Screen: the three URLs stacked, then an arrow from two of them to the preferred one.\]*

You might have:

/service

/service/

/service?source=facebook

or multiple URL variations created by filters, parameters or technical architecture.

Canonicalisation gives search engines a signal about which URL you consider the preferred version.

Again:

signal, not magic command.

*\[Refrain tag: SIGNAL, NOT A SWITCH\]*

SEO is full of these distinctions.

People often look for switches.

Search is much messier than that.

**Redirects and status codes**

You also need to understand HTTP status codes.

*\[Graphic: each code appears as a clean card as it is spoken.\]*

A 200 generally means:

"The request succeeded."

A 301 is a permanent redirect.

A 302 is a temporary redirect.

A 404 means the resource wasn't found.

5xx errors indicate server-side failures.

You don't need to become a server engineer.

But if you're responsible for SEO, you should understand what these things mean.

Because moving a website without understanding redirects can destroy useful pathways into the site.

Changing URLs can break links.

Deleting pages can remove search visibility.

Launching a redesign can introduce technical problems that weren't there before.

This is why technical SEO matters.

It's infrastructure.

**Internal linking**

Now something much less glamorous.

Internal links.

The links between pages on your own website.

These matter for users.

They help people navigate.

And they help search engines discover and understand relationships between pages.

*\[Graphic: a floor plan. Rooms labelled Home, Services, Guides, Contact. Doors appear between them as they are mentioned.\]*

Imagine your website as a building.

Your homepage is one room.

Your service pages are other rooms.

Your guides are other rooms.

Your contact page is another room.

If there are no doors between important rooms, people and crawlers can struggle to move through the building.

Good internal linking creates pathways.

And it also communicates relationships.

A page about phone screen repair might link to:

battery replacement,

water damage repair,

charging port repair,

pricing,

and relevant guides.

That's not keyword stuffing.

That's information architecture.

**Information architecture**

This is another phrase worth understanding.

Information architecture.

It simply means organising information so that it makes sense.

Your website should have a logical structure.

People should be able to understand:

Who you are.

What you do.

Where you operate.

What you sell.

What problems you solve.

How your services relate to one another.

How to contact you.

And search engines should be able to discover those relationships too.

This is why SEO isn't just a marketing activity.

It can expose weaknesses in the underlying structure of a business website.

**Mobile**

Now let's talk about mobile.

*\[B-roll: someone searching on a phone in a shop, in a parked car, walking down a street.\]*

Your customer is often not sitting at a desk.

They're standing in a shop.

Sitting in a car.

Walking down a street.

Holding a phone.

Searching quickly.

Your page needs to work properly on that device.

Text needs to be readable.

Buttons need to be usable.

Forms need to work.

Navigation needs to make sense.

Content shouldn't jump around unnecessarily.

And the main information shouldn't be buried under a mountain of pop-ups.

Mobile isn't a special SEO trick.

It's simply part of building a website for the way people actually use the web.

#### Layer two: content

*\[Layer card: 2 / CONTENT\]*

Now we're at the part most people think SEO is.

Content.

And content matters enormously.

But content does not mean:

"Write 2,000 words because Google likes long articles."

There is no universal ideal word count.

Sometimes the best answer is 200 words.

Sometimes it is 2,000.

Sometimes it is a calculator.

Sometimes it's a table.

Sometimes it's a product page.

Sometimes it's a video.

Sometimes it's a map.

The right amount of content is:

the amount needed to satisfy the user's need properly.

If someone wants your opening hours, don't make them read 2,000 words.

If someone wants to understand a complicated legal process, 200 words might be nowhere near enough.

Content length should follow the problem.

Not a superstition.

**Headings**

Then there are headings.

H1.

H2.

H3.

These help structure the content.

But don't turn heading levels into some kind of SEO ritual.

The purpose is clarity.

A page about laptop repair might have:

*\[Screen: the heading outline builds as spoken.\]*

H1 — Laptop Repair in Leeds

H2 — Common Laptop Problems

H2 — Screen and Keyboard Replacement

H2 — How Much Does Laptop Repair Cost?

H2 — When Is a Laptop Worth Replacing?

That creates structure.

It makes the page easier to scan.

And it helps communicate the organisation of the information.

The goal isn't:

"How many keywords can I put in H2 tags?"

The goal is:

Can a human understand this page quickly?

**Keyword stuffing**

Let's kill another old idea.

You do not need to repeat your target phrase endlessly.

If you're writing:

"Laptop repair Leeds"

and every paragraph contains:

*\[Screen: the phrase highlighted in every paragraph of a page, multiplying.\]*

"laptop repair Leeds"

"laptop repair Leeds"

"laptop repair Leeds"

the page starts sounding like it was written for an algorithm rather than a human.

Search engines have much more sophisticated ways of understanding language and content than simple exact-match repetition.

Write naturally.

Use the terminology your customers actually use.

Cover the subject properly.

Answer related questions.

Explain the service.

And make the page genuinely useful.

**What about blogging?**

Let's talk about blogging because this is where businesses get sold some nonsense.

You don't need a blog because:

"Google likes websites that blog."

You need useful content because your customers have questions.

Those questions can become content.

Suppose you offer data recovery.

People might ask:

Can you recover files from a laptop that won't turn on?

How much does data recovery cost?

How long does it take?

Can photos be recovered from a phone that's been in water?

Is my data kept private while you work on it?

How do I stop it happening again?

Those are legitimate information needs.

Create genuinely useful answers.

But don't publish:

"10 reasons why our repair shop is amazing."

every Tuesday.

Content should exist for a reason.

**Images**

Now images.

Images are content.

And they can also create problems.

A huge uncompressed image can slow a page.

A poorly named image can provide little useful context.

An image without appropriate alternative text can create accessibility problems.

And an image that's important to understanding the page shouldn't be treated as if it doesn't matter.

Think about:

File size.

Dimensions.

Format.

Compression.

Responsive images.

Descriptive filenames where useful.

Alt text.

Captions where useful.

Surrounding text.

And, above all, relevance.

If the image is a photograph of a cracked iPhone screen being repaired, the filename shouldn't necessarily be:

*\[Screen: the two filenames side by side.\]*

IMG\_839201.jpg

If you can meaningfully describe it, something like:

iphone-screen-repair-leeds.jpg

is more informative.

But don't turn alt text into:

"best iPhone screen repair Leeds phone repair shop Leeds."

Alt text is not a keyword dumping ground.

Its primary job is to communicate the image's purpose or content when the image can't be perceived.

**Video**

⁺And the same thinking applies to video.

⁺A video is content too.

⁺Give it a clear title and a description that says what it covers.

⁺Put it on a page where it's genuinely relevant.

⁺And give it captions or a transcript, which we'll come to shortly, so the substance isn't locked inside the audio.

#### Layer three: performance

*\[Layer card: 3 / PERFORMANCE\]*

Now we're going to talk about performance.

Because a website can have excellent information and still be painful to use.

*\[On screen: Source: Google Search Central, "Understanding page experience in Google Search results"\]*

Google's current documentation says its core ranking systems use a variety of signals related to page experience, and specifically that Core Web Vitals are used by ranking systems. But Google also says good Core Web Vitals do not guarantee top rankings, and warns against chasing a perfect score purely for SEO.

That's exactly how I want you to think about this.

*\[Text on screen.\]*

Don't optimise the score.

Optimise the experience.

**Core Web Vitals**

The current Core Web Vitals are:

*\[Graphic: three cards, each with a one-line plain-English meaning.\]*

LCP — Largest Contentful Paint

This is about how quickly the main content becomes visible.

INP — Interaction to Next Paint

This is about responsiveness to interactions.

CLS — Cumulative Layout Shift

This is about unexpected movement of the page.

These are useful because they translate technical behaviour into things users actually experience.

*\[Screen: a phone. The page loads, a button is tapped and responds, then a badly built page jumps as an advert loads above the text.\]*

A page loads.

The customer sees the main content.

They tap a button.

The site responds.

They start reading.

The page doesn't suddenly jump because an advert or image loaded above what they were reading.

That's real user experience.

**PageSpeed Insights**

So use PageSpeed Insights.

*\[Screen: a PageSpeed Insights report on a demo site.\]*

Use Lighthouse.

Use your browser's developer tools when necessary.

And use real-user data where available.

But don't become obsessed with getting a perfect laboratory score.

A perfect laboratory score is not the purpose of your website.

A customer doesn't care whether your Lighthouse report says 97.

They care whether the page loads.

Whether they can read it.

Whether the button works.

Whether the form works.

Whether they can find your phone number.

Whether the website doesn't feel broken.

Whether they can actually buy or book.

That is the objective.

#### Layer four: accessibility

*\[Layer card: 4 / ACCESSIBILITY\]*

Accessibility deserves its own layer.

*\[Screen: a keyboard-only user tabbing through a form, focus ring visible. Then a screen reader reading a well-labelled form.\]*

Can somebody navigate your website using a keyboard?

Can they understand the headings?

Are forms labelled properly?

Is there sufficient contrast?

Can a screen reader make sense of the page?

Are images described appropriately when necessary?

Are interactive elements usable?

Are videos captioned?

Can people tell where focus is?

Can someone complete your contact form without needing perfect vision, perfect hearing, perfect dexterity and perfect motor control?

These are not merely SEO tricks.

They're quality-of-website questions.

Don't sell accessibility as:

"Google gives you extra ranking points if you add alt text."

That's not the right model.

Build an accessible website because people use your website.

And because a website that communicates clearly to a broader range of people is simply a better website.

**Captions**

Captions are another useful example.

A caption can give an image context.

It can tell someone what they're looking at.

For video, captions are even more important.

They make spoken content accessible to people who can't or don't want to hear the audio.

They can also improve comprehension.

And a transcript can make the substance of a video available as text.

But again:

Don't turn accessibility features into fake SEO tricks.

Do them because they make the content better.

Good SEO and good accessibility often overlap because both are pushing you toward clearer, more understandable content.

But they're not identical systems.

**Readability and Flesch**

Now let's talk about readability.

You may have heard of the Flesch Reading Ease score or Flesch-Kincaid.

These formulas attempt to estimate how easy text is to read using characteristics such as sentence length and word length.

They can be useful editorial tools.

But don't turn them into another SEO superstition.

There is no magic rule saying:

"Get your Flesch score to 72 and Google will rank you."

Instead, ask:

Can people understand this?

Are the sentences unnecessarily complicated?

Are paragraphs too long?

Are headings useful?

Can somebody scan the page?

Are technical terms explained?

Can a normal customer understand what we're saying?

Sometimes expert content needs technical language.

That's fine.

The answer isn't to dumb everything down.

The answer is to explain it properly.

#### Layer five: trust

*\[Layer card: 5 / TRUST\]*

**E-E-A-T**

This brings us to E-E-A-T.

*\[Graphic: the four words, one at a time.\]*

Experience.

Expertise.

Authoritativeness.

Trustworthiness.

You'll hear E-E-A-T everywhere in modern SEO.

But here's what I don't want you to do.

Don't turn it into:

"Google gives me an E-E-A-T score of 73."

That's not how you should think about it.

Think about what those ideas represent.

Experience:

Have you actually done the thing?

Expertise:

Do you understand it?

Authoritativeness:

Do other credible signals support your authority in the subject?

Trustworthiness:

Can people reasonably trust the information and the organisation behind it?

And trust is particularly important.

Who wrote this?

Why should I believe it?

Can I verify it?

Does the business actually exist?

Are there real contact details?

Are there genuine examples?

Are claims supported?

Does the site make it clear who is responsible for the information?

That's much more useful than trying to game a fictional score.

**First-hand experience**

Experience is particularly interesting.

Suppose I'm writing about a phone that won't charge.

I can produce an article from generic information.

Or I can explain:

"We see this fault regularly."

"Very often, it's pocket lint packed into the charging port."

"Here's what happened on a recent job."

"Here are the three things we check first."

"Here's the point where you should stop troubleshooting and bring it in."

That's first-hand experience.

It can make content more useful because you're bringing something to the page that isn't simply a rewrite of everything already online.

And this is one reason businesses have a huge advantage if they actually document what they do.

*\[Slow down. This is the line business owners should take away.\]*

Your real work contains information.

Your jobs contain questions.

Your customers contain questions.

Your staff contain expertise.

Your mistakes contain lessons.

Your projects contain evidence.

Your case studies contain experience.

That is content.

**Author SEO**

This brings us to authors.

If you're publishing expert material, especially in areas where expertise matters, don't automatically hide the person who wrote it.

Give the reader context.

Who is the author?

What do they do?

What experience do they have?

Why are they qualified to discuss this?

If appropriate, create an author page.

Link the author to their profile.

Use accurate author information and appropriate structured data where supported.

But don't manufacture credentials.

If someone has ten years of experience, say that if it is true.

If they have a qualification, say what it is if relevant.

If they don't have specialist credentials, don't invent them.

SEO works much better when you stop thinking about how to look authoritative and start thinking about how to be useful and trustworthy.

#### Layer six: conversion

*\[Layer card: 6 / CONVERSION\]*

⁺And the last layer of the door is the last of the six stages from earlier. Clicked is not converted.

Here's another simple principle.

Don't make the visitor work to discover whether they're in the right place.

If someone searched:

"cracked iPhone screen repair Leeds"

and your page starts with:

*\[Screen: a page that opens with a wall of company history above the fold.\]*

"Welcome to our website. We are a family-run company founded in…"

you've made them wait.

Tell them what the page is about.

Then give them the information they need.

Then establish credibility.

Then explain the service.

Then answer questions.

Then make the next step obvious.

SEO and conversion optimisation overlap here.

Because getting someone to the page is only half the job.

### Part Nine: Measuring it

*\[Chapter card: PART NINE / MEASURING IT. Corner badge: all five places lit.\]*

#### Search Console

Now let's talk about one of the most important free tools available to a website owner.

Google Search Console.

*\[Screen: Search Console overview on a demo or permitted property. Blur any client data.\]*

If you own a website and you don't have Search Console configured, you're missing one of the most useful windows into how Google sees your site.

*\[On screen: Source: Search Console Help, "About Search Console"\]*

Google describes Search Console as a tool for monitoring and understanding a site's performance in Google Search, including crawling, indexing and performance.

Inside it, you can investigate things such as:

*\[Screen: the cursor moves through each report as it is named.\]*

Queries.

Impressions.

Clicks.

Click-through rate.

Average position.

Pages.

Indexing.

URL inspection.

Sitemaps.

Core Web Vitals.

Search appearance.

And other reports depending on the site.

#### Impressions

*\[Screen: Performance report, impressions line highlighted.\]*

An impression is essentially an opportunity for your result to have appeared in a search result.

You can have impressions without clicks.

That's useful.

Because it tells you:

Google is showing this page for something.

Now ask:

What query?

What position?

What page?

Is the result attractive?

Is the title clear?

Is the page actually answering the intent?

#### Clicks

Clicks tell you that somebody selected your result.

Now you've moved one step further.

But clicks still aren't customers.

You can have 10,000 clicks and terrible business results.

Or 100 highly relevant clicks and excellent business results.

That's why SEO metrics should eventually connect to business outcomes.

Calls.

Bookings.

Leads.

Sales.

Applications.

Downloads.

Whatever the business actually needs.

#### CTR

CTR is click-through rate.

Broadly:

How often did people click compared with how often the result was shown?

It's useful.

But don't reduce SEO to:

"Get the CTR as high as possible."

Context matters.

A high CTR on the wrong audience isn't necessarily useful.

And a lower CTR for a highly specific query may still produce excellent customers.

*\[Text on screen.\]*

Metrics are evidence.

They're not the objective.

#### Average position

Then there's average position.

This one gets misunderstood constantly.

People want:

"What is my position?"

But search results vary.

Queries differ.

Locations differ.

Devices differ.

Search features change.

Personalisation and context can matter.

So average position is a measurement across a set of impressions, not a permanent physical position engraved into Google.

Don't treat it as:

"My website is number 4."

Treat it as:

"Across this data set, this is the reported average position."

That's much more accurate.

#### URL Inspection

Search Console's URL Inspection tool can be incredibly useful.

*\[Screen: URL Inspection on a single URL, showing index status.\]*

You can investigate a specific URL.

Is it indexed?

What does Google report?

Are there issues?

Can you request indexing where appropriate?
